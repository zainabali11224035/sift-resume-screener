Guidelines

How Sift scores candidates, and where it falls short.

How scoring works

Every candidate gets an overall score out of 100, made of two parts:

  • Skill match (70% of the score) — the share of the job's required skills that appear in the resume, checked against a fixed skills list.
  • Text similarity (30% of the score) — how closely the overall wording of the resume matches the job description, using TF-IDF and cosine similarity.

Skill match is weighted higher because it's the part a recruiter can verify at a glance. Text similarity catches relevant experience that isn't captured by an exact skill name.

Supported file types

  • .pdf — text-based PDFs only. Scanned or image-only PDFs will not extract any text (no OCR is performed).
  • .docx — standard Word documents.
  • .txt — plain text resumes.

What the parser extracts

  • Name — assumed to be the first line of the resume that isn't an email or phone number. This is a heuristic and can be wrong for unusually formatted resumes.
  • Email and phone — matched via pattern recognition; may miss unusual formats.
  • Skills — matched against a fixed, editable list of ~60 common technical and professional skills, plus common synonyms/aliases (e.g. "React.js"/"ReactJS" → React, "JS" → JavaScript, "Amazon Web Services" → AWS, "k8s" → Kubernetes). A skill not in this list — and not recognized as an alias of one — will never be detected, even if the candidate has it.
  • Years of experience — only detected when stated explicitly, e.g. "5 years of experience." Experience implied by a work history timeline is not currently calculated.
  • Education, certifications & links — resumes are split into sections (Summary, Experience, Education, Skills, Projects, Certifications) by heading detection. From these, Sift best-effort extracts degree/institution/year lines, a certifications list, and LinkedIn/GitHub/portfolio links, shown under each candidate on the Results page. This depends on the resume using recognizable section headings — unconventional layouts may not split cleanly.

Known limitations

  • This is a first-pass filter, not a hiring decision. A low score does not mean a candidate is unqualified — it means the resume's wording didn't match the job description well.
  • The skills dictionary (and its synonym list) is fixed at build time. Skills specific to a niche field, or unlisted alternate phrasings, may not be recognized.
  • Name extraction is a best-effort guess and should be spot-checked for resumes with unconventional layouts (e.g. two-column templates).
  • Education/certifications/links extraction relies on the resume having clear section headings; resumes without them, or with headings buried mid-paragraph, won't be split correctly.
  • Text similarity is pure word-overlap (TF-IDF) — it has no real language understanding, so synonymous phrasing outside the skills list (e.g. "built REST APIs" vs. "developed backend services") isn't recognized as related.
  • Sift does not verify claims made in a resume — it only measures textual overlap with the job description.

Data handling

Uploaded resume files are processed in memory for scoring and deleted immediately after. Only the extracted fields (name, email, phone, skills, scores) are stored, in a local database on the machine running Sift.
